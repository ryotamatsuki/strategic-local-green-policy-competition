\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:results}

The central object is the response of jurisdiction $A$'s infrastructure policy to jurisdiction $B$'s firm-specific green subsidy,
\[
\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}.
\]
The headline comparison holds product-market substitutability fixed and changes only the curvature of the territorial emissions-target loss. The rival's infrastructure is held fixed, while jurisdiction $A$ reoptimizes both its own subsidy and infrastructure. The existing rivalry-indexed sign crossing at $d=2$ is treated below as a complementary characterization, not the sole contribution.

\subsection{A general cross-instrument decomposition}

Let
\[
H_A=
\begin{pmatrix}
W_{s_As_A}&W_{s_Ah_A}\\
W_{s_Ah_A}&W_{h_Ah_A}
\end{pmatrix}
\]
be government $A$'s own-policy Hessian on an interior duopoly branch. Differentiating its two first-order conditions with respect to $s_B$ gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}
\partial s_A^{BR}/\partial s_B\\[2pt]
\partial h_A^{BR}/\partial s_B
\end{pmatrix}
=-H_A^{-1}
\begin{pmatrix}
W_{s_As_B}\\W_{h_As_B}
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:br-ift}
\end{equation}
Under strict concavity, $\det H_A>0$, so
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}
=\frac{W_{s_Ah_A}W_{s_As_B}-W_{s_As_A}W_{h_As_B}}{\det H_A}.
\label{eq:cross-instrument-general}
\end{equation}
The numerator combines a direct cross effect, $W_{h_As_B}$, with an indirect term transmitted through the interaction between the local government's two instruments. A sign reversal is possible when product-market rivalry changes their relative strength.

Write $s=s_A$, $h=h_A$ and $b=s_B$, holding $h_B$ fixed. Eliminating $ds/db$ from $W_s=W_h=0$ yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}
=-\frac{W_{hb}-W_{hs}W_{sb}/W_{ss}}
        {W_{hh}-W_{hs}^{\,2}/W_{ss}}.
\label{eq:profiled-infrastructure}
\end{equation}
The denominator is negative under $H_A\prec0$. Consequently the sign of the response is the sign of the \emph{profiled} mixed effect in the numerator, not necessarily the sign of $W_{hb}$. A rival subsidy can raise the marginal return to infrastructure when the local subsidy is held fixed yet reduce infrastructure after that subsidy is optimally adjusted. This distinction is testable within the exact same policy game; it is not itself a claim that profiling a Hessian is a new mathematical method.

As a complementary comparison, Table~\ref{tab:profiled-channels} shows the same decomposition when $d=2$ is fixed and rivalry instead changes from $\theta=.5$ to $.9$. This is not the fixed-$\theta$ result of Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed-theta-target}. The channel contributions use the \emph{same} total-government $W_{hs}/W_{ss}$ and Schur-complement denominator; each welfare component is evaluated at the same underlying branch, rather than separately reoptimized. The direct mixed effect $W_{hb}$ is positive at these **two stated rivalry points**, while the fully reoptimized response changes sign. It can be negative at smaller positive rivalry values. At higher rivalry, the target-loss contribution grows more strongly than the opposing producer-surplus term, and the consumer-surplus contribution turns positive. These values demonstrate the source of the canonical reversal, not a universally dominant mechanism.

\subsection{A fixed-rivalry comparison of territorial-target discipline}
\label{sec:fixed-theta}

The model's precise public-finance implication is that two otherwise identical economies facing the \emph{same intensity of product-market competition} may have opposing infrastructure reactions to the same normalized rival competitiveness shock. We fix $\theta=31/40$ and all primitives other than the territorial-target loss curvature $d$ at the rational values displayed in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The level $\bar E$ and the weight $d$ are distinct: the marginal loss with respect to emissions is $-d(E-\bar E)$, and $d$ governs its curvature. We consider $d_-=39/20$ and $d_+=41/20$. Neither $d$ nor the two government profiles is a measured empirical policy treatment.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-rivalry target-discipline response reversal]
\label{prop:fixed-theta-target}
At $\theta=31/40$, with
\[
(k_x,k_g,\mu,\nu,\kappa,e,\beta,\xi,m,\bar E)
=(4,18,9/10,6/5,4/5,11/10,17/10,1/10,2,0),
\]
there exist symmetric pure-strategy subgame-perfect equilibria of the full nonnegative-action game at \emph{each} of $d=39/20$ and $d=41/20$. In each specified equilibrium, both governments choose strictly positive subsidies and infrastructure, both firms produce positive quantities, and each government's policy is its strictly optimal global best response to the other's policy. Holding the rival infrastructure fixed, the own-subsidy-reoptimized infrastructure response to a marginal rival subsidy satisfies
\[
\left.\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}\right|_{d=39/20}<0,
\qquad
\left.\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}\right|_{d=41/20}>0.
\]
At both points $W_{h_As_B}>0$ when the own subsidy is held fixed. The comparisons persist in some nonempty, possibly different open primitive neighborhoods of the two points. The proposition does not assert that every intermediate $d$ is globally admissible, that the entire $d^*(\theta)$ boundary is a global-equilibrium curve, or that all government equilibria are unique.
\end{proposition}

The two directions are not a consequence of holding the local subsidy fixed: its direct effect is positive in \emph{both} economies. Instead, the own subsidy--infrastructure substitution embedded in \eqref{eq:profiled-infrastructure}, combined with the target-loss curvature, produces opposite \emph{profiled} slopes. Because rival policies enter the active branch through $y_B=(\mu/k_g)s_B+\nu h_B$, a marginal change in rival infrastructure, normalized to the same $\Delta y_B$, has the same local response. This does not identify a subsidy-specific causal transmission mechanism.

\emph{Proof and scope.} At each of the two rational points, solving the symmetric policy FOCs gives the strictly positive policies in Table~\ref{tab:fixed-theta-target}. The own-policy Hessian is strictly negative definite, and the two-government symmetric FOC Jacobian is nonsingular. Independently maximizing each of the five exact government objective pieces over nonnegative policies, including their faces and vertices, leaves the interior duopoly candidate strictly optimal. The rival-kink and rival-monopoly regions are unreachable at either point; the best own-kink and own-monopoly challengers coincide at their common boundary and are worse by the strictly positive rational $\Delta W$ shown in the table. These are exact finite, coercive quadratic optimization problems, not grid searches. Their full rational certificates, boundary constraints, and independent test path are in Appendix~\ref{app:global-equilibrium} and the Stage-4A certification record. Positive strict inequalities, active-regime margins and invertible Jacobians then yield a continuously varying local equilibrium and global best-response mapping by the implicit-function theorem and continuity of constrained maxima. The opposite derivative signs therefore hold in two separate nonempty open neighborhoods. Nothing in this argument connects those neighborhoods across all $d$.

\subsection{Complementary reversal as rivalry varies}


For $\mu>0$ in the model of Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:equilibrium}, symbolic reduction yields a positive function $\Omega(\theta)$ and a quartic
\begin{equation}
P(u)=A_4u^4+A_3u^3+A_2u^2+A_1u+A_0,
\qquad u\equiv\theta^2,
\label{eq:P-def}
\end{equation}
such that
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}
=-\Omega(\theta)P(\theta^2),
\qquad \Omega(\theta)>0
\quad\text{for }\theta\in(0,1].
\label{eq:P-sign}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $A_j$ are explicit functions of the primitives $(k_x,k_g,\mu,\nu,d,e,\beta,\xi)$; intercept terms such as $a-c$ and the emissions-target level $\bar E_i$ drop out of the best-response slope. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} records the reduction and the repository contains an independent symbolic verification. The case $\mu=0$ is treated separately in Corollary~\ref{cor:mu-zero}, because the rival subsidy then has no product-market transmission through green investment.

Define the Bernstein coefficients of $P'$ on $[0,1]$ as
\begin{align}
B_0&=A_1,\\
B_1&=A_1+\frac23A_2,\\
B_2&=A_1+\frac43A_2+A_3,\\
B_3&=A_1+2A_2+3A_3+4A_4.
\label{eq:bernstein-derivative}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Unique instrument-switching threshold]
\label{prop:threshold}
Suppose $\mu>0$, the firm-stage regularity and government strict-concavity conditions hold on an interior duopoly best-response branch, and suppose
\begin{equation}
P(0)>0,\qquad P(1)<0,
\qquad B_0,B_1,B_2,B_3<0.
\label{eq:threshold-conditions}
\end{equation}
Then there exists a unique $\theta^*\in(0,1)$ such that
\begin{equation}
0<\theta<\theta^*
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}<0,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\theta^*<\theta\leq1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}>0.
\end{equation}
If, in addition, that interior branch is the true global policy best response on an open neighborhood, the same derivative is the derivative of the global best response there.
\end{proposition}

The sign change is a directional effect, not a discrete decision to abandon one instrument for another. The interior formula alone does not certify a global policy response; the kink- and monopoly-deviation checks in Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium} remain essential.

The proof is simple once the equilibrium has been reduced to \eqref{eq:P-sign}. The conditions $B_0,\ldots,B_3<0$ imply $P'(u)<0$ throughout $[0,1]$ because the cubic Bernstein basis is nonnegative on that interval. Combined with $P(0)>0>P(1)$, this yields a unique root $u^*\in(0,1)$ and hence $\theta^*=\sqrt{u^*}$. At $\theta=0$ the cross-jurisdictional response is zero rather than negative.

\subsection{Economic mechanism}

A higher $s_B$ raises firm $B$'s green investment. When $\mu>0$, that investment also affects marginal production cost and product-market quantities. The resulting business-stealing effect changes $A$'s output and both of its endogenous investment choices. The feedback strength is summarized by
\[
R=\frac1{k_x}+\frac{\mu^2}{k_g}.
\]
Product substitutability changes how this investment feedback propagates through quantities, but the final government response is not a pure competitiveness-preservation effect. Equation~\eqref{eq:cross-instrument-general} combines consumer-surplus, producer-surplus, territorial-target, and within-government policy-interaction terms.

For comparison with the profiled decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:profiled-channels}, the \emph{direct} mixed derivative at $\theta=.9$ (holding the local subsidy fixed) is
\[
W_{h_As_B}
=0.001638_{\,CS}
-0.039629_{\,PS}
+0.046078_{\,\text{target}}
=0.008087.
\]
It differs from the profiled component values because the latter also incorporate the induced adjustment of $s_A$. At this point $E_{A,h_A}\simeq0.952>0$ and $E_{A,s_B}\simeq-0.0242$, so the rival subsidy reduces local production and territorial emissions while additional local infrastructure raises emissions on net through its scale effect. The target contribution is therefore substantial here, not a theorem of target-term dominance throughout the admissible parameter space. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} checks the target-intensity restriction explicitly.

The interior reduced system also implies that rival policies enter jurisdiction $A$ through
\[
y_B=\frac{\mu}{k_g}s_B+\nu h_B.
\]
Hence, whenever the same interior branch applies,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial z_A^{BR}}{\partial h_B}
=\frac{\nu k_g}{\mu}
\frac{\partial z_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B},
\qquad z_A\in\{s_A,h_A\}.
\label{eq:rival-policy-proportionality}
\end{equation}
The rival-subsidy shock is therefore a convenient cross-instrument normalization, not a uniquely identified transmission channel.

\subsection{Matched benchmark without conventional competitive investment}

Consider the nested benchmark in which conventional investment is removed, equivalently $1/k_x\rightarrow0$. Let $\bar P(u)$ denote the resulting cross-instrument polynomial and let $C_0,\ldots,C_4$ be its degree-four Bernstein coefficients.

\begin{proposition}[Matched no-conventional-investment benchmark]
\label{prop:no-x}
Suppose the benchmark is interior and strictly concave. If
\[
C_0,C_1,C_2,C_3,C_4>0,
\]
then
\[
\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}<0
\qquad\forall\theta\in(0,1].
\]
Hence the sign reversal is absent from that benchmark region.
\end{proposition}

For the canonical parameter values, the no-$x$ expression is proportional to
\begin{equation}
602500u^2-3281550u+3486659,
\label{eq:no-x-witness}
\end{equation}
which is strictly positive on $u\in[0,1]$. The full and nested models therefore generate qualitatively different best-response networks at the same canonical environmental and policy primitives.

The dashed curve in Figure~\ref{fig:threshold-response} visualizes this matched benchmark separation: at the same canonical remaining primitives, the no-$x$ response stays below zero for all positive rivalry levels.

This is a matched benchmark separation, not a universal necessity result. Holding the other primitives fixed but changing $\beta$ to $1.3$ produces a verified no-$x$ global-equilibrium example in which the response is negative at $\theta=.9$ and positive at $\theta=1$. Conventional investment can therefore create a reversal that is absent under the matched canonical benchmark, but it is not necessary for every possible reversal.

\begin{corollary}[Competitiveness link]
\label{cor:mu-zero}
If $\mu=0$, green investment has no effect on product-market competitiveness. A rival subsidy then changes rival abatement but not rival output through this channel, and
\[
\frac{\partial s_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}
=
\frac{\partial h_A^{BR}}{\partial s_B}=0.
\]
\end{corollary}

Finally, the threshold has economically interpretable local comparative statics. Around the canonical region,
\[
\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial\nu}<0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial k_x}>0.
\]
Thus, locally, switching occurs at weaker product-market rivalry when shared infrastructure is more productive or when firms can adjust conventional competitive investment more flexibly. These derivatives are local results rather than global monotonicity claims.
