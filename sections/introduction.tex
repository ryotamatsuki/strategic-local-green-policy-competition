\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

When a rival jurisdiction raises its green-investment subsidy, should a local government expand or contract productive infrastructure? This question concerns the local government's joint best response in subsidies and infrastructure, holding the rival's infrastructure fixed. Green industrial policy is increasingly place based. Governments can support firm-level decarbonizing investment, and policy packages can also include shared inputs---clean-power access, utility networks, ports, industrial land, grid capacity, and related infrastructure---that affect local production conditions. Japan's GX Strategic Area framework illustrates this policy architecture by combining place-based industrial-cluster and infrastructure measures with a separate firm-facing investment-support channel \citep{METIGX2026,METIGXSubsidy2026}. Subnational decarbonization objectives can also be policy relevant; the OECD documents a substantial subnational role in Japan's green transition \citep{OECDJapan2025}. These choices operate in markets where firms compete across jurisdictional boundaries. A policy adopted in one region can therefore change not only emissions and local investment, but also the product-market position of firms located elsewhere.

A rival subsidy can either crowd out local infrastructure or induce a government to reinforce shared productive capacity. We ask whether product-market substitutability changes the sign of $\partial h_A^{BR}/\partial s_B$, where jurisdiction $A$ reoptimizes $(s_A,h_A)$ in response to $B$'s subsidy, holding $h_B$ and the model primitives fixed. The derivative concerns one government's conditional best response, not the movement of the two jurisdictions' entire policy-equilibrium vectors. We call a sign change in this derivative ``switching''; it is not a discrete choice between instruments.

Our contribution identifies when product-market competition changes the direction of a cross-instrument policy response. Earlier work establishes strategic interactions between business taxes and productive public inputs \citep{HauptmeierMittermaierRincke2012,BenassyQuereGobalrajaTrannoy2007}, shows how fiscal competition and infrastructure interact with oligopoly \citep{MoritaOkoshi2025}, and studies conventional and green investment within the same firm \citep{StrandholmEspinolaMunoz2025}. Building on these ingredients, we characterize a unique product-substitutability threshold at which a government's infrastructure response to a rival green-investment subsidy changes sign. Unlike the FDI and infrastructure comparison in \citet{MoritaOkoshi2025}, our comparison holds plant locations fixed and follows the same cross-instrument best response as product substitutability changes. We also establish global equilibrium for the canonical parameter vector and a nonempty neighborhood around it, and show that a matched model without conventional investment has no reversal at the same remaining canonical parameters.

We study two jurisdictions, each hosting one fixed-location firm. Local governments first choose a firm-specific green-investment subsidy and productive green public infrastructure. Firms then choose conventional cost-reducing investment and green investment, followed by differentiated Cournot competition. Green investment can both reduce emissions and affect productivity, while infrastructure can affect production costs and territorial emissions. The model contains no hard government budget linking the two policies and no firm-level investment constraint linking conventional and green investment.

The main result is a competition-induced sign reversal. On an interior duopoly branch, primitive sufficient conditions reduce the cross-instrument best-response derivative to the sign of a quartic polynomial in $\theta^2$. Bernstein-basis conditions make that polynomial strictly decreasing, yielding a unique threshold $\theta^*$. The global-equilibrium issue is treated separately: with nonnegative policies, investments, and quantities, sufficiently aggressive policies can change market structure. We therefore solve the duopoly, limit-pricing-kink, and monopoly continuations and show that the canonical rational witness supports a true global policy SPNE on a nonempty open neighborhood. At that witness, $\theta^*\simeq0.774$.

The mechanism involves both the direct infrastructure incentive and the adjustment of the government's own subsidy. A rival subsidy can lower local production and territorial emissions, making infrastructure expansion less costly under the local emissions target, while also eroding local producer surplus. The government adjusts its two instruments jointly; this own-subsidy interaction can overturn a positive direct cross effect. At the canonical moderate-rivalry point $\theta=.5$, the direct infrastructure--rival-subsidy cross derivative is positive, but the induced adjustment of $s_A$ makes the full infrastructure best-response slope negative. At $\theta=.9$, the positive direct contribution dominates and infrastructure rises; at this reported point the government also reduces its subsidy. These component signs describe the canonical calibration, not a theorem on their signs for all primitives.

The matched benchmark clarifies the role of conventional investment. Holding the remaining canonical parameters fixed, removing that investment margin eliminates the reversal. This comparison identifies a way in which endogenous conventional investment changes the policy-response pattern; it is not a general necessity result, since other admissible parameters produce a reversal even without conventional investment. If green investment has no productivity effect, by contrast, the rival subsidy does not transmit through product-market quantities.

The welfare analysis is secondary. Decentralized governments do not internalize the effects of their policies on rival-region consumer surplus, producer surplus, and territorial emissions objectives, so decentralized and coordinated decisions generally differ. In the canonical example coordination raises total model welfare and increases the fiscal weight of infrastructure, but it also raises output and territorial emissions. The result is therefore a second-best regional-welfare comparison, not a theorem that coordination necessarily produces lower emissions.

The institutional mapping remains deliberately limited. The Japanese programs cited above motivate the coexistence of place-based productive infrastructure and firm-facing green investment support, but they do not imply that Japanese local governments literally control both instruments as specified here. The model is a stylized interjurisdictional-policy game rather than a literal mapping of legal authority or financing incidence.
