\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Two jurisdictions can face the same rival green-investment policy and equally intense competition between their firms, yet find it optimal to adjust productive public infrastructure in opposite directions. Why? A rival's subsidy strengthens its firm's competitive position, which can reduce the case for local infrastructure. At the same time, lower local output changes a government's valuation of deviations from its territorial emissions target. A government that can adjust its own firm-facing subsidy as well as infrastructure does not have to transmit the rival shock through infrastructure alone. The sign of the resulting infrastructure response is therefore a question about the \emph{joint public-policy choice}, not merely about business stealing.

We hold constant product substitutability and the rival government's infrastructure and ask how government $A$ changes its infrastructure when government $B$ marginally raises its green-investment subsidy. Crucially, $A$ reoptimizes \emph{both} its own subsidy and infrastructure. The response is a \emph{partial best-response derivative} to the rival subsidy, not the effect of both governments changing all policies in a new equilibrium, not a difference in infrastructure levels across fiscal regimes, and not an empirical causal estimate. On the active continuation, the two rival instruments enter the same competitiveness index, $y_B=(\mu/k_g)s_B+\nu h_B$. Hence the subsidy shock is a normalization of an external competitive-policy perturbation, not a distinct subsidy-only transmission mechanism.

Our central result gives a precise two-economy comparison. Fix product substitutability at $\theta=31/40=0.775$ and all other primitives at a rational benchmark except for $d$, the curvature of a quadratic loss from deviations from the territorial emissions target. When $d=39/20=1.95$, the infrastructure best-response derivative is approximately $-0.00150230$; when $d=41/20=2.05$, it is approximately $+0.00140289$. At both points, the \emph{direct} cross effect with the local subsidy held fixed is positive. The opposite directions arise only after the local subsidy is reoptimized. Importantly, these are not merely stationary interior reactions. At each rational witness, the specified symmetric duopoly configuration is a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the full nonnegative-action game: we compare the government optimum with all feasible kink, monopoly, and boundary deviations. The strict non-duopoly welfare gaps are approximately $1.22504$ and $1.25098$, and the result persists locally around each witness. We do \emph{not} prove that every intermediate target weight generates a global equilibrium or that the entire algebraic switching boundary is feasible.

The underlying game has three stages. Governments simultaneously choose firm-facing green-investment subsidies $s_i$ and productive shared infrastructure $h_i$; fixed-location firms then choose conventional cost-reducing and green investment; finally they compete in nonnegative differentiated Cournot quantities. The government values local producer surplus, its half of integrated-market consumer surplus, the cost of infrastructure, and the squared distance of territorial emissions from a target. The parameter $d$ is a \emph{loss curvature}, not the numerical target itself: the marginal welfare loss with respect to emissions is $d(E_i-\bar E_i)$ in absolute-penalty notation, with the sign reversed in the government's objective. As the firm's output and green investment respond to a rival subsidy, the consumer, producer, and target-loss components can offset one another. The own-subsidy first-order condition changes their effective balance in the infrastructure equation.

The distinction from the closest literature is narrower than the presence of multiple policy instruments or any sign reversal. \citet{HauptmeierMittermaierRincke2012} establish simultaneous tax--public-input responses and a sign-changing own-tax response to a rival public input in a mobile-capital setting. \citet{MoritaOkoshi2025} already derive conditional changes in equilibrium infrastructure under fiscal competition, an equilibrium subsidy response to prior infrastructure, and product-similarity-dependent effects. \citet{StrandholmEspinolaMunoz2025} analyze conventional and green private R\&D under environmental regulation. The new comparison here holds product competition \emph{fixed}, varies only the local target-loss curvature, and shows opposite, subsidy-reoptimized \emph{infrastructure derivatives at two fully certified global policy equilibria}. This does not invent the inverse-Hessian formula or the general possibility of sign-switching. Its possible value is to identify when a government's environmental objective changes the direction of a genuine, rather than infeasible, competitive-policy reaction.

Figure~\ref{fig:threshold-response} displays this complementary $d=2$ rivalry comparison, with a certified full-model response and a no-conventional-investment interior diagnostic. The traditional comparison varying product substitutability instead of $d$ remains informative but secondary. At the canonical $d=2$, the derivative switches from negative to positive at the unique \emph{positive-rivalry sign-changing root} $\theta^*\simeq0.7738043861$, under a separate global certificate. At $\theta=0$ it is also zero because the product markets do not interact. The rival subsidy and infrastructure share the same reduced competitive index; conventional investment is not universally required for a sign reversal. Moreover, the exact interior sign boundary $d^*(\theta)$ is not a universal diagram of implemented global equilibria. For example, an interior candidate at $d=1.5$ can be defeated by firm-exclusion deviations and at $d=3$ can imply negative infrastructure. These counterexamples matter for the policy interpretation of any sign diagram.

The result speaks to public economics because productive infrastructure and business support are joint fiscal decisions when private investment and environmental objectives interact across markets. It does not show that real prefectures have authority over this exact instrument pair, that the latent target-loss curvature can be inferred directly from a published emissions target, or that policy coordination necessarily reduces emissions. In the model, a feasible coordinated active-duopoly benchmark raises measured regional welfare and territorial emissions together. Japan's GX programs illustrate the coexistence of common productive infrastructure and green business support \citep{METIGX2026,METIGXSubsidy2026}, while subnational decarbonization initiatives motivate territorial objectives \citep{OECDJapan2025}; neither establishes the model's institutional assumptions.
