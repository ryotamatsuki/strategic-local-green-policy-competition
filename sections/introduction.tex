\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Suppose one jurisdiction expands subsidies for firms' green investment. A neighboring government could respond by reducing its own productive infrastructure, because its firms now face a stronger competitor, or by investing more in shared productive capacity to defend their position. Even the direction of that response is not obvious. The difficulty is particularly acute when infrastructure and firm-specific subsidies operate through different margins of the same product market. A rival policy may change not only the profitability of an incumbent but also the investments through which that firm subsequently competes. This paper asks when intensified product-market rivalry changes the \emph{sign} of a government's infrastructure response to its rival's green subsidy.

Two forces pull in opposite directions. Stronger rival investment can erode local production and producer surplus, weakening the incentive to expand infrastructure. But the same loss of local output can relax a territorial emissions target, thereby changing the marginal cost of productive infrastructure to the government. Local consumer surplus and the interaction between a government's own subsidy and infrastructure choices matter as well. The relative importance of these forces changes as products become closer substitutes. Consequently, there is no general reason to expect either fiscal retrenchment or policy matching to describe the entire range of rivalry.

We establish a conditional, sharper prediction. Under sufficient regularity and sign conditions, the derivative of the local infrastructure best response with respect to a rival green-investment subsidy changes from negative to positive exactly once as product substitutability increases. For an explicitly certified rational parameter configuration, the threshold is $\theta^*\simeq0.774$. The distinction between an interior calculation and a valid policy equilibrium is substantive: a government can sometimes deviate far enough to change the product-market regime. We therefore verify the full nonnegative duopoly, kink, and monopoly continuations and establish that the canonical response belongs to a genuine global subgame-perfect equilibrium over the rivalry range and on a nonempty open neighborhood of primitives. This is an existence and local-domain result, \emph{not} a global equilibrium characterization for arbitrary admissible parameters. Figure~\ref{fig:threshold-response} visualizes this certified canonical sign crossing beside the matched no-conventional-investment benchmark; it is an illustration of the theorem's domain, not evidence of universal switching.

To obtain the result, we consider two jurisdictions, each with a fixed-location firm. Governments simultaneously choose a firm-facing green-investment subsidy, $s_i$, and productive green public infrastructure, $h_i$. Firms then choose conventional cost-reducing investment, $x_i$, and green investment, $g_i$, before competing in differentiated quantities. Green investment has both an environmental role and, when $\mu>0$, a productivity channel; infrastructure improves local production conditions and may affect territorial emissions. These distinct private investment margins transmit a rival subsidy into the local government's two-dimensional policy problem. The analysis does not impose a hard joint public budget or allow plants to relocate.

The claim is not that governments have never reacted across fiscal instruments. \citet{HauptmeierMittermaierRincke2012} already analyze taxes and productive public inputs as strategic substitutes or complements in jurisdictional competition. Nor is the combination of fiscal incentives, infrastructure, Cournot competition, and threshold effects new: \citet{MoritaOkoshi2025} study when fiscal competition changes infrastructure in a model involving foreign-investment location and transport costs. Joint conventional and green R\&D is also studied by \citet{StrandholmEspinolaMunoz2025}. What differs here is the \emph{object of the prediction}: a single cross-instrument \emph{best-response derivative} crosses zero as product substitutability changes, after endogenous investment adjustment and subject to a certified global-equilibrium bridge. This is a deliberately narrow theorem-level distinction, not a claim of novelty for the ingredients or for policy reactions in general.

A matched counterfactual clarifies, but does not overstate, the role of conventional investment. At the same remaining canonical primitives, removing the conventional investment margin eliminates the sign reversal. Other admissible primitives nevertheless permit a reversal even without that margin; dual investment is therefore not universally necessary. Equally, rivalry alone is not sufficient: the territorial-target weight determines whether the opposing policy incentives cross at all. In the interior sensitivity exercise, the response remains negative when $d=1.5$, crosses once when $d=2$, and remains positive when $d=3$. These distinctions matter for interpreting the result as a conditional strategic-response theorem rather than a universal prediction that competition causes more public investment.

The analysis also distinguishes modeled regional welfare from environmental effectiveness. Coordination can raise total modeled welfare while increasing output and territorial emissions in the canonical example; we do not claim that policy coordination necessarily reduces physical emissions. The implications for policy are consequently about \emph{which strategic reaction to investigate}, not a prescription to expand infrastructure or a demonstrated causal effect in observed jurisdictions. Japan's green-transformation policies illustrate the coexistence of place-based infrastructure and firm-directed support \citep{METIGX2026,METIGXSubsidy2026}, while the documented subnational role in the green transition provides additional motivation \citep{OECDJapan2025}. These examples do not establish that Japanese local governments control the two instruments exactly as in this stylized game.
