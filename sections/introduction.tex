\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Green industrial policy is increasingly place based. Subnational governments can support a particular firm's decarbonizing investment, but they can also invest in shared inputs---clean-power access, utility networks, ports, industrial land, grid capacity, and related infrastructure---that affect the competitiveness of a broader local production base. These choices are made in an environment in which firms compete across jurisdictional boundaries. A policy adopted in one region can therefore change not only emissions and local investment, but also the product-market position of firms located elsewhere.

This raises a portfolio question that is different from the standard race-to-the-bottom question. Suppose a rival jurisdiction increases a firm-specific green subsidy. Should a local government match the subsidy, retreat from green support, or respond through a different instrument? We show that the answer can change qualitatively with the intensity of product-market competition. When firms choose both conventional cost-reducing investment and green investment, stronger rivalry can reverse the sign of a local government's infrastructure response to a rival's green subsidy.

We study two jurisdictions, each hosting one fixed-location firm. Local governments first choose a firm-specific green-investment subsidy and shared green public infrastructure. Firms then choose conventional cost-reducing investment and green investment, followed by differentiated Cournot competition. Green investment can both reduce emissions and improve productivity, while public infrastructure can both reduce emissions and lower local production costs. The model deliberately contains no hard government budget linking the two policies and no firm-level investment constraint linking conventional and green investment. Any instrument switching is therefore generated by strategic feedback rather than an accounting identity.

The main result is a competition-induced sign reversal. Under primitive sufficient conditions there exists a unique product-substitutability threshold $\theta^*$ such that a rival's green subsidy reduces local green infrastructure when rivalry is weak but raises local infrastructure when rivalry is strong. The proof reduces the cross-instrument best-response derivative to the sign of a quartic polynomial in $\theta^2$. Bernstein-basis conditions imply that this polynomial is strictly decreasing on the relevant interval, yielding a unique threshold. In the canonical interior witness the threshold is approximately $0.774$. Below it, a higher rival subsidy leads the local government to contract both its own subsidy and infrastructure. Above it, the local government continues to reduce its own firm-specific subsidy but increases shared infrastructure instead.

The economic mechanism works through firms' investment portfolios. A rival subsidy raises the rival firm's green investment. If green investment also improves productivity, the subsidized firm becomes more competitive and expands output. Stronger product substitutability magnifies the resulting business stealing. The local firm then adjusts not only green investment but also conventional cost-reducing investment. This second investment margin changes the relative marginal value of the local government's two policy instruments. At sufficiently high rivalry, shared infrastructure becomes a particularly effective response because it supports local competitiveness without simply matching the rival's firm-specific subsidy.

A nested benchmark identifies the source of the result. Removing conventional competitive investment eliminates one component of the strategic amplification term. Under the corresponding sufficient conditions, the local infrastructure response to a rival subsidy is then negative for every positive level of product substitutability. The sign reversal is therefore not reproduced by a model with only green investment. Likewise, when green investment has no productivity effect, the rival subsidy does not transmit through product-market competition and the cross-jurisdictional response through this mechanism disappears.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it identifies a new result within multi-instrument interjurisdictional competition: product-market rivalry can reverse a cross-instrument government best response. This is narrower than claiming that governments strategically use multiple instruments. That behavior is already central to the fiscal-competition literature; for example, \citet{HauptmeierMittermaierRincke2012} study strategic interaction in business taxes and productive public inputs, while \citet{BenassyQuereGobalrajaTrannoy2007} analyze tax and public-input competition more generally.

Second, the paper links this government-side response to firms' endogenous allocation across conventional and green investment. Simultaneous conventional and green R\&D is itself not new: \citet{StrandholmEspinolaMunoz2025} show that the two investment margins interact with environmental regulation. Our result comes from placing those firm choices inside an interjurisdictional policy game and showing that the additional investment margin changes the qualitative best-response network. The benchmark without conventional investment provides the formal separation.

Third, the paper distinguishes targeted green support from shared green infrastructure in a setting where both can affect competitiveness. \citet{MoritaOkoshi2025} show that fiscal competition and public infrastructure can interact and generate threshold effects in a model of foreign direct investment. Earlier environmental-federalism models also combine local fiscal and environmental choices \citep{OatesSchwab1988,BayindirUpmann1998Emissions}, and work by \citet{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993,MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995} shows how imperfect competition and environmental policy interact with plant-location incentives. We do not claim novelty for subsidy--infrastructure interaction, oligopoly, or policy composition individually. The contribution is the sign reversal generated by the joint endogeneity of local policy instruments, two firm investment margins, and product-market rivalry.

The welfare analysis is deliberately secondary to the mechanism. Decentralized governments do not internalize the effects of their policies on rival-region consumer surplus, producer surplus, and emissions objectives, so decentralized and coordinated portfolios generally differ. In the canonical witness coordination raises total welfare and shifts the portfolio toward shared infrastructure. We treat this as supporting evidence rather than a new general theorem about fiscal decentralization.

The mechanism is relevant to current place-based green industrial policy. Japan's GX Strategic Area framework, for example, combines industrial-location policy with shared infrastructure and decarbonized-energy access, while related programs support firm investment \citep{METIGX2026,METIGXSubsidy2026}. The OECD also emphasizes the role of subnational governments in Japan's green transition \citep{OECDJapan2025}. The model is not calibrated to Japan, but these institutions illustrate why the distinction between firm-specific support and shared green infrastructure is economically meaningful.
