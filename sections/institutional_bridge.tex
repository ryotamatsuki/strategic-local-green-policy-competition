\section{Institutional and Empirical Bridge}
\label{sec:institutional}

The two public instruments have a functional, not literal, policy interpretation. The firm-facing subsidy $s_i$ changes the private return to green investment, while shared productive infrastructure $h_i$ changes the local firm's production conditions and can directly affect territorial emissions. Potential real-world analogues include firm investment incentives and common industrial utilities or clean-power, port, grid, and hydrogen infrastructure. Japan's GX policy initiatives illustrate these complementary margins \citep{METIGX2026,METIGXSubsidy2026}; the OECD documents the role of Japanese subnational actors in implementing environmental objectives \citep{OECDJapan2025}. Neither observation establishes that a particular prefecture can select both modeled instruments independently or bears their complete financial incidence.

The target parameter must be interpreted carefully. The model places a quadratic penalty $d(E_i-\bar E_i)^2/2$ on the distance from a delegated territorial emissions target. Here $d$ is the \emph{curvature of the loss from target deviations}, whereas the marginal objective derivative with respect to emissions is $-d(E_i-\bar E_i)$. A published numerical target $\bar E_i$, a measured level of emissions, and the unobserved curvature $d$ are different objects. The certified fixed-$\theta$ comparison varies $d$ while leaving the market, target level, technologies, and other primitives fixed; it should not be interpreted as the effect of simply announcing a more ambitious emissions target.

\subsection{Conditional empirical diagnostics}

The new comparison suggests a conditional research design rather than a direct calibrated forecast. An observational study would first need two or more markets with comparable product substitution, production technologies and initial policy states, and distinct credible measures of environmental-target loss curvature, or institutionally justified proxies for how the marginal penalty changes with emissions deviations. It would then need an exogenous or credibly instrumented rival green-investment subsidy shock, while accounting for the rival's infrastructure choice and for the local government's simultaneous adjustment of its \emph{own} subsidy and infrastructure. Merely comparing cross-sectional infrastructure levels under different targets or using one positive policy correlation would not test the partial best-response derivative.

On a differentiable continuation, the rival's instruments enter through the common index
\[
y_B=\frac{\mu}{k_g}s_B+\nu h_B.
\]
Under the same initial market and policy conditions, shocks that generate the same change in $y_B$ predict the same local response even if split differently between rival subsidies and infrastructure. In particular, $\Delta s_B$ with $\Delta h_B=0$ is equivalent, locally, to a rival infrastructure change $\Delta h_B=(\mu/\nu k_g)\Delta s_B$ with $\Delta s_B=0$. The original policy units are not comparable without this normalization. At a kink or under government-policy indifference, the statement concerns the \emph{best-response sets} rather than a unique derivative; changes of continuation or equilibrium selection invalidate the smooth-index comparison.

To measure the relevant response, researchers would have to distinguish an externally induced marginal policy perturbation from simultaneous adjustments in all governments' policies in a new equilibrium. Product-substitutability estimates, appropriately classified public-input expenditures, credible policy exposure, independent local budget rules, the emissions and target-loss schedule, and the active-firm regime are all needed. The same-$\theta$ sign contrast is predicted \emph{only} for parameter configurations inside the certified local global-equilibrium neighborhoods, not across an unspecified distribution of actual jurisdictions. A failure to match the equal-index response within those maintained conditions would contradict the model; the manuscript does not supply such empirical evidence.

The historical rivalry-threshold comparison at $d=2$, its no-$x$ benchmark, and the dependence on infrastructure productivity and private-investment adjustability remain complementary local predictions. They do not expand the new fixed-rivalry two-point theorem to unrestricted parameters. Institutional budget coupling, plant relocation, central-local cost sharing, policy anticipation, and endogenous compliance targets remain outside the baseline, where these marginal predictions may need substantial modification.
