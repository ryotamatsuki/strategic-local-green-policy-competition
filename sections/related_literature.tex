\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

The relevant comparison is not whether fiscal policy, infrastructure, or green investment have previously appeared in the same game. It is whether an existing model already establishes a conditional \emph{cross-instrument reaction}: a local infrastructure best-response derivative to a rival green subsidy that changes sign exactly once with product substitutability, in an endogenous-investment continuation whose global policy equilibrium is certified on a specified domain. Three neighboring strands delimit that claim.

First, multi-instrument interjurisdictional competition and cross-policy reactions are established parts of public economics. \citet{BayindirUpmann1998PublicGoods} study competition involving taxes and industrial public goods, while \citet{BenassyQuereGobalrajaTrannoy2007} examine taxation and productive public inputs. Particularly close is \citet{HauptmeierMittermaierRincke2012}: governments choose business taxes and productive public inputs, and a change in a neighboring fiscal instrument can induce responses in both own instruments. Their reaction-function logic is thus prior art for the two-dimensional government problem, including an infrastructure response to a rival fiscal change. Our result does not discover that cross-instrument responses exist. It isolates a different, conditional inference: product substitutability in differentiated oligopoly changes the \emph{sign of the same derivative} through investment and territorial-policy feedback, and the response is connected to a globally admissible policy equilibrium on a certified region. The distinction is in the comparative-static object, not the number of policies chosen.

Second, recent infrastructure and fiscal-competition theory already contains thresholds and imperfect product-market competition. \citet{MoritaOkoshi2025} combine infrastructure provision, fiscal incentives, multinational location decisions, and Cournot competition, and identify circumstances in which fiscal competition changes infrastructure investment. This is an important overlap, not a remote analogy: one cannot claim that infrastructure, subsidies, Cournot competition, or threshold effects are individually new. Their central comparison concerns the infrastructure consequences of fiscal competition across a location and trade-cost environment. The present object is the signed \emph{simultaneous cross-best-response slope} with fixed-location incumbents and endogenous firm investment; its threshold variable is product substitutability. Whether that narrower distinction is sufficiently important for publication is an editorial judgment, not something that follows merely from different timing or notation.

Third, environmental and industrial-organization theory has long recognized strategic effects of regulation and endogenous firm adjustment. \citet{OatesSchwab1988} study local policy competition with environmental quality; \citet{BayindirUpmann1998Emissions} treats interjurisdictional emission-tax competition under imperfect competition. \citet{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993,MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995} emphasize market-structure and plant-location changes, a warning against interpreting an interior candidate as a global equilibrium. More recently, \citet{StrandholmEspinolaMunoz2025} allow firms to choose both conventional cost-reducing and green R\&D, and analyze their interaction with environmental regulation. We therefore do not claim that two private investment margins, or their interaction under oligopoly, are novel. They matter here because they transmit a rival's subsidy into a \emph{different jurisdiction's} jointly chosen subsidy and infrastructure response.

The paper also speaks to place-based industrial policy. \citet{SlatteryZidar2020} document firm-specific regional incentives; these motivate the firm-facing subsidy margin without requiring that plant location be chosen within the game. In contrast to location-bidding models, fixed location is deliberate and limiting: the result is not a theorem about where firms locate or which jurisdiction attracts a mobile plant. Nor is it an empirical test of observed green-program reactions.

These strands imply a constrained contribution. Existing work supplies the policy-instrument interaction, oligopolistic investment adjustment, environmental externalities, and threshold vocabulary. What the certified model adds is an explicit one-time, rivalry-indexed reversal in a cross-jurisdiction \emph{infrastructure-response derivative}, under specified equilibrium and primitive restrictions. For the remaining canonical primitives, removing conventional investment eliminates the reversal, although a separate admissible no-$x$ example shows that conventional investment is not necessary in general. Neither the global continuation repair nor the algebraic existence of a polynomial threshold is independently offered as broad new fiscal-competition theory. The value of the result rests on the economically meaningful but \emph{model-specific} conditional policy-response distinction.
