\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

This paper concerns a particular \emph{marginal policy reaction}, not the general discovery that governments adjust fiscal instruments jointly or that policy reactions can switch signs. The closest studies already demonstrate both mechanisms. Other tax-and-public-input competition models also establish that governments' jointly selected policies alter cross-border fiscal incentives \citep{BenassyQuereGobalrajaTrannoy2007}. The relevant comparison is therefore the responding instrument, the rival policy held fixed, the parameter that changes, and whether the sign belongs to an admissible policy equilibrium rather than an unimplemented first-order-condition candidate.

In a mobile-capital setting, \citet{HauptmeierMittermaierRincke2012} allow a local government to set a business tax and productive public input simultaneously. The publicly available ECB working paper (WP 1033, Sect.~2, equation (7)) differentiates both government first-order conditions, just as in our \eqref{eq:br-ift}; WP equations (10)--(11) yield $\partial g_i/\partial t_j=-4/(3bk^2)<0$ for public inputs responding to a rival tax and $\partial t_i/\partial g_j=-(bk^2-4)/(3bk^2)$ for \emph{own tax} responding to rival inputs. The latter changes sign as production curvature passes $bk^2=4$. The accessible typeset journal formulation labels the corresponding general and symmetric reaction expressions (A.1), (7), and (8). Thus a multi-instrument inverse-Hessian reaction, a shared rival tax-base direction, and a curvature-dependent sign change are \emph{prior results}. Our comparison instead holds product substitutability fixed and changes territorial-target-loss curvature; it measures \emph{own infrastructure} responding to a marginal \emph{rival firm-facing subsidy}, after \emph{own} subsidy readjustment. Different variables do not establish novelty by themselves; the additional information is that opposite signs occur at two certified full-policy equilibria with identical product rivalry. The exact original WP equations are directly accessible; a full independent check of all typeset journal-version proofs is not asserted.

\citet{MoritaOkoshi2025} are particularly close in the combination of fiscal competition, productive infrastructure, transport, and Cournot product-market rivalry. Their Proposition~1 compares \emph{equilibrium infrastructure levels with and without fiscal competition} and finds a trade-cost threshold. Their Sect.~3.1 and footnote~20 derive a host-government subsidy response to \emph{its own prior infrastructure} in a sequential location-bidding game; that is not the inverse of our cross-jurisdictional infrastructure response to an exogenous rival subsidy. Their Sect.~4.3 and official Online Appendix~E already show that \emph{product similarity} can alter the sign of the fiscal-competition effect on nonhost infrastructure levels. We do not claim that rivalry-induced infrastructure changes or threshold effects were unknown. The present result compares two targets at the same product substitutability while keeping rival infrastructure fixed and permitting a joint local adjustment; the two fiscal regimes and strategic timing cannot be identified by relabeling derivatives.

On the firm side, \citet{StrandholmEspinolaMunoz2025} already study cost-reducing and green research investments, with a single regulator setting an emissions fee \emph{after} firms' investments in their baseline timing. The author-distributed full manuscript, publisher abstract, and journal metadata confirm the important joint-investment channel and the comparison without conventional investment. The final publisher typesetting and every supplementary theorem could not be independently checked against that manuscript. In our game two governments choose their subsidies and infrastructure \emph{before} private investment; those changes of timing and policy object define the comparison but do not make either investment margin intrinsically new. Our own no-conventional-investment counterexample also excludes a claim that two R\&D types are universally necessary for a sign change.

The environmental-policy competition literature provides further constraints. \citet{OatesSchwab1988} examine competition across jurisdictions with environmental objectives; \citet{BayindirUpmann1998Emissions} examine regional emissions-tax competition among imperfectly competitive local firms, and \citet{BayindirUpmann1998PublicGoods} consider industrial public goods. The bibliographic record of the emissions-tax paper is verified, but its complete original reaction-function theorem was unavailable for equation-level comparison; no inference of priority follows from that source gap. \citet{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993,MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995} demonstrate why market structure and plant-location changes cannot be ignored when assessing global environmental-policy incentives. \citet{SlatteryZidar2020} document regional firm incentives, which motivates the empirical policy distinction without making this a location-choice model or an identified empirical estimate.

The retained finding is deliberately narrow. With fixed plants and a reduced rival competitiveness index $y_B$, changing the \emph{curvature of a territorial-target deviation loss}, while holding product substitution and all other primitives fixed, can reverse the direction of a joint government-policy best response at two strict global equilibria. This is stronger than plotting an arbitrary interior IFT numerator but weaker than a general model-free policy sign criterion, an empirical causal effect, or a welfare theorem. Its potential interpretation is as a conditional diagnostic for which mechanism can dominate in a specifically defined public-policy game; the appropriate market and institutional measurements remain outside the paper.
