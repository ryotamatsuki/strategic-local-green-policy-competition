\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

The closest comparison is how productive infrastructure reacts to a rival fiscal instrument when the local government also adjusts its own policies. Multidimensional strategic responses are already established in fiscal competition; our narrower object is a particular cross-instrument best-response derivative and how its sign varies with product substitutability under stated conditions. The analysis draws together environmental federalism, public-input competition, place-based incentives, and investment under oligopoly.

First, the environmental-federalism literature studies how decentralized jurisdictions combine fiscal and environmental policies. \citet{OatesSchwab1988} show that local governments can choose capital taxes or subsidies together with environmental quality when competing for industry. \citet{BayindirUpmann1998Emissions} studies interjurisdictional emission-tax competition when local firms compete imperfectly, while \citet{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993} and \citet{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995} emphasize the interaction among environmental regulation, oligopoly and plant-location incentives. These studies establish that policy-induced changes in market structure can matter for equilibrium. In our setting, this observation motivates comparisons between duopoly and exclusionary continuations when assessing a government's global policy response.

Second, the fiscal-competition literature has long recognized that governments compete with both firm-facing fiscal instruments and productive public inputs. \citet{BayindirUpmann1998PublicGoods} compares tax and public-expenditure competition with industrial public goods. \citet{BenassyQuereGobalrajaTrannoy2007} analyze taxation and public inputs, while \citet{HauptmeierMittermaierRincke2012} study strategic interactions in business taxes and productive public inputs. \citet{LevaggiPanteghini2021} establish that heterogeneous tax-reaction signs can arise from heterogeneous public-expenditure spillovers; sign heterogeneity by itself is therefore not a novelty claim here. Their work is particularly relevant because a change in a neighboring fiscal instrument can elicit adjustments in both local taxes and public inputs. We examine a different comparative-static question: how varying product substitutability changes the sign of the infrastructure response to the same rival green-subsidy shock. The unique threshold is established under explicit primitive conditions and, in the canonical region, is associated with the global policy best response.

A recent contribution by \citet{MoritaOkoshi2025} combines fiscal competition for foreign direct investment with transport infrastructure and shows that competition can raise infrastructure provision under particular transport-cost conditions. Although both models connect fiscal incentives, infrastructure and product-market competition, their comparative-static objects differ. Morita and Okoshi analyze fiscal competition and infrastructure incentives around foreign direct investment. Here we vary product substitutability while following one cross-instrument best-response derivative through firms' endogenous investment choices. The matched-parameter comparison helps identify how conventional investment changes the derivative's sign pattern.

Third, the place-based-policy literature documents the prevalence and consequences of firm-specific incentives. \citet{SlatteryZidar2020} describe state and local business incentives and show that large discretionary subsidies are an important instrument of regional economic policy. This evidence motivates treating targeted firm support as a meaningful policy margin, but our model keeps plant locations fixed in order to isolate the product-market and investment channels rather than bidding for discrete location.

Fourth, environmental industrial organization studies firms' innovation and abatement decisions under market competition and regulation. \citet{StrandholmEspinolaMunoz2025} allow firms to invest simultaneously in conventional cost-reducing R\&D and green R\&D and show that these margins interact with environmental regulation. That interaction provides a firm-level channel through which a rival subsidy can affect output and the strategic incentives of local governments.

Together, these literatures suggest an interaction between market rivalry and the composition of local green policy. When firms adjust conventional and green investment in response to policies, increasing product substitutability can change whether a rival subsidy induces a jurisdiction to expand or reduce its productive infrastructure. The unique-crossing result, its global-equilibrium qualification around the canonical parameters, and the matched investment benchmark specify the circumstances under which this change occurs. The counterexample in Section~\ref{sec:results} also shows why the benchmark comparison cannot be interpreted as a universal requirement for conventional investment.
