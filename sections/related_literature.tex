\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

The relevant comparison is not whether fiscal policy, infrastructure, or green investment have previously appeared in the same game. It is whether an existing model already establishes a conditional \emph{cross-instrument reaction}: a local infrastructure best-response derivative to a rival green subsidy that changes sign exactly once with product substitutability, in an endogenous-investment continuation whose global policy equilibrium is certified on a specified domain. Three neighboring strands delimit that claim.

First, multi-instrument interjurisdictional competition and cross-policy reactions are established parts of public economics. \citet{BayindirUpmann1998PublicGoods} study competition involving taxes and industrial public goods, while \citet{BenassyQuereGobalrajaTrannoy2007} examine taxation and productive public inputs. Particularly close is \citet{HauptmeierMittermaierRincke2012}, who simultaneously model a business tax and productive public input under competition for mobile capital. Their available ECB working-paper formulation (WP 1033, Sect.~2, equations (7)--(12)) already contains the own-policy inverse-Hessian reaction formula; it yields $\partial g_i/\partial t_j=-4/(3bk^2)<0$ for the public-input response to rival taxes and $\partial t_i/\partial g_j=-(bk^2-4)/(3bk^2)$ for the \emph{own tax} response to rival public inputs. Thus the latter derivative changes sign at $bk^2=4$. Sign changes in a multi-instrument fiscal best response and shock-direction proportionality are accordingly prior art, not general contributions here. The paper's different, narrow object is the \emph{own infrastructure} derivative to a rival firm-facing subsidy, indexed by product substitutability and a territorial-target objective. The equation numbering above refers to the 2009 ECB working-paper version; the final journal article's formula-by-formula equivalence has not been independently checked.

Second, recent infrastructure and fiscal-competition theory already contains thresholds and imperfect product-market competition. \citet{MoritaOkoshi2025} combine infrastructure provision, fiscal incentives, multinational location decisions, and Cournot competition, and identify circumstances in which fiscal competition changes infrastructure investment. This is an important overlap, not a remote analogy: one cannot claim that infrastructure, subsidies, Cournot competition, or threshold effects are individually new. Their Proposition~1 compares equilibrium infrastructure levels \emph{with and without fiscal competition} and obtains a threshold in infrastructure-independent transport costs; Sect.~3.1 and footnote~20 also give the host government's \emph{equilibrium subsidy response to its own infrastructure}, $\partial s_B^*/\partial k_B=(17\tau-2a)/9$, whose sign changes with transport conditions. Moreover, Sect.~4.3 and official Online Appendix~E compare differentiated-product endpoints and show that similarity changes the fiscal-competition effect on \emph{non-host infrastructure levels}. Those comparisons anticipate both ambiguous policy-interaction signs and a role for product similarity. They do not, however, identify the derivative $\partial h_A^{BR}/\partial s_B$ while rival infrastructure is held fixed and the local subsidy is simultaneously reoptimized. Reversing $\partial s_B^*/\partial k_B$ is not a valid substitution for that derivative, nor can an across-regime level difference be treated as a partial best response. The remaining distinct object must still demonstrate economic value, not just mathematical non-identity.

Third, environmental and industrial-organization theory has long recognized strategic effects of regulation and endogenous firm adjustment. \citet{OatesSchwab1988} study local policy competition with environmental quality; \citet{BayindirUpmann1998Emissions} treats interjurisdictional emission-tax competition under imperfect competition. \citet{MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1993,MarkusenMoreyOlewiler1995} emphasize market-structure and plant-location changes, a warning against interpreting an interior candidate as a global equilibrium. More recently, \citet{StrandholmEspinolaMunoz2025} allow firms to choose both conventional cost-reducing and green R\&D and analyze their interaction with an emissions regulator. The author-distributed manuscript and publisher record show a timing in which firm investment precedes a single regulator's emissions-fee choice and Cournot output, unlike our two governments' simultaneous policies preceding private investment. Thus the two investment margins and their endogenous adjustment are not themselves novel. Our nonnecessity counterexample also prevents treating two investment types as the minimal architecture for any sign reversal. The publisher's final proof/appendix has not been fully reconciled against the author manuscript, so this comparison cannot alone close the prior-art gate.

The paper also speaks to place-based industrial policy. \citet{SlatteryZidar2020} document firm-specific regional incentives; these motivate the firm-facing subsidy margin without requiring that plant location be chosen within the game. In contrast to location-bidding models, fixed location is deliberate and limiting: the result is not a theorem about where firms locate or which jurisdiction attracts a mobile plant. Nor is it an empirical test of observed green-program reactions.

These strands imply a constrained contribution. Existing work supplies the policy-instrument interaction, oligopolistic investment adjustment, environmental externalities, and threshold vocabulary. What the certified model adds is an explicit one-time, rivalry-indexed reversal in a cross-jurisdiction \emph{infrastructure-response derivative}, under specified equilibrium and primitive restrictions. For the remaining canonical primitives, removing conventional investment eliminates the reversal, although a separate admissible no-$x$ example shows that conventional investment is not necessary in general. Neither the global continuation repair nor the algebraic existence of a polynomial threshold is independently offered as broad new fiscal-competition theory. The value of the result rests on the economically meaningful but \emph{model-specific} conditional policy-response distinction.
