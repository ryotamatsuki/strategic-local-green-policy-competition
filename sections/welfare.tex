\section{Welfare and Coordination}
\label{sec:welfare}

The main contribution concerns strategic instrument switching, not a generic decentralization inefficiency. Nevertheless, the model delivers a useful welfare benchmark because the decentralized governments internalize different margins from a coordinated policymaker.

For the benchmark used below, suppose the two jurisdictions are constrained
to choose the same nonnegative policies, $s_A=s_B=s$ and $h_A=h_B=h$.
Define the \emph{symmetric-policy restricted coordination benchmark} by
\begin{equation}
\max_{s,h\geq0}
\left[W_A(s,h,s,h)+W_B(s,h,s,h)\right].
\label{eq:planner}
\end{equation}
The firms' investment and output decisions are re-solved at these policies.
Under the maintained $m>0$ and $R<3/4$ conditions, symmetric
nonnegative policies retain both firms in the active-duopoly continuation.
This is an optimum within the symmetric-policy restriction, \emph{not}
the solution to the unrestricted four-policy planner problem.
As a separate diagnostic, for any policy $z_A\in\{s_A,h_A\}$, the marginal externality omitted from the decentralized first-order condition can be written as
\begin{equation}
\Omega_{z_A}^{W}
\equiv
\frac{\partial W_B}{\partial z_A}.
\label{eq:welfare-wedge-def}
\end{equation}
Using the structure of $W_B$,
\begin{equation}
\Omega_{z_A}^{W}
=
\frac12\frac{\partial CS}{\partial z_A}
+
\frac{\partial PS_B}{\partial z_A}
-
d(E_B-\bar E_B)\frac{\partial E_B}{\partial z_A}.
\label{eq:welfare-wedge}
\end{equation}
The wedge therefore contains three real cross-jurisdictional effects: consumer-surplus effects, producer-surplus/business-stealing effects, and emissions-target effects. The welfare comparison is not generated by subsidy transfers alone.

The sign of each wedge is parameter dependent, so the theory does not imply a global ranking of each decentralized policy instrument relative to its coordinated counterpart. For the canonical parameter vector, Table~\ref{tab:welfare} reports the selected symmetric decentralized global-SPNE policy and the symmetric-policy restricted coordination benchmark. The latter is not a globally optimal allocation over unrestricted jurisdiction-specific policies: asymmetric policies can induce monopoly and yield strictly higher aggregate model welfare. In particular, at $\theta=0.9$, the feasible policy vector $(s_A,h_A,s_B,h_B)=(30,18,0,0)$ induces an $A$-monopoly continuation and aggregate welfare approximately $10.158$, greater than the restricted symmetric benchmark's $3.720$. This comparison is not Pareto-improving: one jurisdiction incurs a large welfare loss. No global planner solution or interregional-compensation mechanism is characterized here.

Because $s$ is a subsidy rate while $h$ is an infrastructure quantity, the ratio $h/(s+h)$ has no meaningful unit interpretation and is not used. For a descriptive fiscal-composition measure, define
\begin{equation}
\phi_h
\equiv
\frac{\kappa h^2/2}{s g+\kappa h^2/2}.
\label{eq:fiscal-infra-share}
\end{equation}
This compares subsidy outlay $sg$ with the infrastructure resource/outlay term in common units. It is not a welfare share because the subsidy transfer cancels from baseline regional welfare.

At $\theta=0.9$, total welfare is approximately $2.187$ at the exhibited decentralized global-SPNE equilibrium and $3.720$ at the symmetric-policy restricted coordination benchmark. The fiscal infrastructure share rises from $0.363$ to $0.692$. However, coordination also raises each region's output from approximately $1.184$ to $2.397$ and territorial emissions from approximately $0.907$ to $1.634$.

This last comparison is important for interpretation. Coordination is not an emissions-reduction theorem. The coordinated policymaker internalizes cross-jurisdictional consumer-surplus, producer-surplus, and territorial-target effects, and in the canonical example the production gain is large enough that the coordinated solution tolerates higher territorial emissions. The exercise is therefore a \emph{restricted second-best regional-welfare illustration}, not an unrestricted planner optimum, a welfare ranking across all potential policy equilibria, or a claim that more coordination necessarily produces greener physical outcomes.

Nor should these numbers be interpreted as a recommendation for globally maximizing carbon policy. The model's emissions term represents territorial or delegated decarbonization objectives. A global climate externality depending on $E_A+E_B$ would introduce an additional coordination motive. We deliberately leave that global-damage extension outside the baseline so that the paper isolates the strategic interaction between local green industrial policy and product-market rivalry.
