\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central policy object is the \emph{partial}, jointly reoptimized best response to a rival competitiveness shock. The first-order-condition decomposition in Equation~\eqref{eq:profiled-infrastructure} and Table~\ref{tab:profiled-channels} show why a positive direct marginal value of local infrastructure need not imply a positive infrastructure response: the local government also adjusts its own green subsidy. At the canonical two comparison points the producer-surplus effect becomes more negative with product rivalry, while the territorial-target contribution grows still more and the consumer-surplus contribution changes sign. This is an account of the model's conditional response, not a new generic theorem about two-policy Hessians or an assertion that a single channel invariably dominates.

Identification is more demanding than observing correlated regional expenditures. On the interior branch, the rival's subsidy and infrastructure enter through the common competitiveness index $y_B=(\mu/k_g)s_B+\nu h_B$. For $\mu,\nu>0$, holding one rival instrument fixed while perturbing the other and adjusting the local government's two instruments gives $\partial h_A^{BR}/\partial s_B=(\mu/k_g)\partial h_A^{BR}/\partial y_B$ and $\partial h_A^{BR}/\partial h_B=\nu\,\partial h_A^{BR}/\partial y_B$. Two rival-policy packages delivering the same change in $y_B$ must induce the same local response \emph{when the same continuation and equilibrium selection apply}; at kinks or with policy indifference this becomes a best-response-\emph{set} statement. A valid empirical comparison requires instrument-specific, exogenous rival shocks or credible predetermined exposure, comparable units of the policy index, product-substitutability measures and an independently interpretable local infrastructure series. Simultaneous endogenous changes in both governments' policy levels cannot be mistaken for the partial derivative in the theorem.

The role of environmental objectives is likewise conditional. The weight $d$ represents a marginal welfare loss from missing a territorial target, \emph{not} the observed quantity of emissions or a government's published numerical target alone. Within the canonical interior quadratic representation, a decreasing threshold $d^*(\theta)$ separates negative from positive profiled responses. But the global-equilibrium and policy-feasibility tests are an essential filter: at $\theta=.9$, $d=1.5$ admits a profitable firm-exclusion deviation and $d=3$ produces a negative interior infrastructure candidate. The rigorously certified sign reversal remains the rational canonical witness and a nonempty open primitive neighborhood, not the whole $(\theta,d)$ sensitivity plane. This distinction can guide which theoretical or empirical comparisons might be diagnostic without pretending to certify unimplemented policies.

Institutional transfer also requires checking the policymaker's legal and financial instruments. Plants are fixed, the canonical governments are symmetric, infrastructure is a scalar public input, and no binding common budget, national carbon-damage function or vertical intergovernmental transfer is modeled. Japan's GX programs motivate the coexistence of productive shared infrastructure and firm-directed green support but do not establish that a prefectural government sets both instruments independently or can generate the exogenous rival shocks required above. Observed reactions might instead reflect central financing rules, project timing, mobile-firm bidding, or common shocks. Hence the canonical threshold number $0.774$ has no direct empirical cutoff interpretation for an actual industry or jurisdiction without structural estimation of the model's parameters and market.

Finally, the welfare exercise answers a narrower question than climate coordination. The \emph{feasible coordinated active-duopoly benchmark} has higher modeled total regional welfare and higher territorial emissions than the specified decentralized global SPNE; it is neither the unrestricted climate-planner optimum nor proof that every coordinated choice raises emissions. The main conclusion is that strong rivalry can conditionally change how one government responds to a normalized rival policy shock. Whether the resulting restricted, target-sensitive diagnostic adds enough to existing fiscal-competition theory is a separate journal-level contribution question, not one that a new exposition or more precise numerical threshold can settle by itself.
