\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main result is a controlled comparison across two economies, not an unconditional relationship between product-market rivalry and infrastructure expenditure. At the \emph{same} substitutability $\theta=31/40$, a jurisdiction facing a marginal rival green-policy shock can reduce infrastructure when the territorial target-loss curvature is $d=39/20$ and expand infrastructure when it is $d=41/20$. In both specified full-policy equilibria the direct infrastructure--rival subsidy cross derivative is positive. Eliminating the government's own subsidy first-order condition yields a different, \emph{profiled} cross effect: the negative producer-surplus contribution is offset by a target-loss contribution, with the own-instrument adjustment altering their weights. This is why treating the local subsidy as fixed can miss the change of direction. The economic distinction lies in the opposite infrastructure responses at two globally admissible policy profiles that differ only in target-loss curvature, rather than in the algebra of the Schur complement itself.

The fact that the two policy vectors are strictly optimal against all nonnegative deviations is essential for that interpretation. A local stationary quadratic optimum can lose to a policy that induces a rival-firm production shutdown; it can also require negative infrastructure. The $\theta=.9,d=1.5$ and $d=3$ calculations illustrate those failures. At $\theta=1,d=d^*(1)$ the sign-zero interior candidate is defeated by a monopoly-inducing deviation. Hence an interior target-weight boundary can organize algebraic signs without tracing the global policy-equilibrium correspondence. Our strictly certified points and their separate open neighborhoods support a substantive existence comparison; they do not certify a broad target-weight interval, all equilibria, or the whole $d^*(\theta)$ curve.

The external policy experiment must also be defined precisely. On any differentiable active branch the rival subsidy and infrastructure enter the reduced continuation through $y_B=(\mu/k_g)s_B+\nu h_B$. Holding rival infrastructure constant while increasing its subsidy is one normalized way to perturb this competitive index. It does not isolate a subsidy-specific causal channel, and a simultaneous equilibrium shift in all four government policies is not the partial derivative in Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed-theta-target}. For the same initial state, equal changes in $y_B$ imply identical best-response maps on the same branch; with indifference or kinks the statement concerns policy-response sets. A test would require exogenous rival-policy variation and careful measurement of initial exposure, product-market substitution, and government instruments. No empirical causal effect is estimated here.

The territorial objective is a reduced-form \emph{curvature of the loss from emissions-target deviations}. Its weight $d$ is not the observed target $\bar E$; the associated marginal welfare derivative is $-d(E-\bar E)$. Interpreting target intensity as an observed number of tonnes would be incorrect. Actual local authority over green subsidies and common infrastructure, central financing, public budgets, plant mobility, and transboundary emissions damages can also alter the strategic problem. The feasible coordinated active-duopoly comparison in Section~\ref{sec:welfare} yields higher modeled regional welfare and \emph{higher} physical territorial emissions than the specified decentralized equilibrium, so the sign reversal offers no automatic climate-improvement prescription.

Finally, existing tax--public-input and environmental-competition research already contains multi-instrument response equations, ambiguous strategic signs, and product-competition-dependent infrastructure outcomes. The restricted additional implication here is that two different environmental objective curvatures generate opposite, subsidy-reoptimized infrastructure reactions while rivalry and other primitives are held fixed, and that a full nonnegative-game equilibrium check is needed before interpreting either sign. A general empirical policy ranking, a globally identified target boundary, and unrestricted fiscal or climate-welfare conclusions remain outside the proven result.
