\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central economic inference concerns \emph{how a jurisdiction reacts}, not whether either policy instrument is desirable in isolation. A rival's green-investment subsidy changes its firm's cost position, both firms' investment choices, and product-market quantities. The local government must then value the resulting change in consumer surplus, locally captured producer surplus, territorial emissions, and the interaction of its own subsidy and infrastructure. As products become closer substitutes, the relative weights of these effects can change enough to reverse the infrastructure best-response slope. This is a conditional strategic response: it is not discrete switching from one policy to another, and it does not imply that infrastructure spending rises monotonically with rivalry.

The environmental target is part of the mechanism rather than an innocuous preference parameter. The exact interior sensitivity comparison, holding other canonical primitives fixed, is instructive: at $d=1.5$ the cross-infrastructure response is negative throughout positive rivalry, at $d=2$ it crosses zero once, and at $d=3$ it remains positive. Thus greater substitutability alone is insufficient to generate a reversal; target discipline determines whether the competing incentives exchange dominance. At the canonical high-rivalry point, the territorial-target component of the direct cross effect is material. An explanation based solely on \emph{defending local competitiveness} misses this environmental-objective channel.

The result also does not identify a unique \emph{rival subsidy} transmission channel. On the interior reduced system, rival policies affect local continuation incentives through $y_B=(\mu/k_g)s_B+\nu h_B$. A change in rival infrastructure therefore enters the same market-transmission index at a different scale. The subsidy derivative is a convenient cross-instrument object, not evidence that subsidy shocks operate independently of infrastructure shocks. Likewise, conventional firm investment matters for the matched canonical comparison but is not universally necessary: an admissible benchmark without conventional investment reverses under different environmental parameters. These are informative negative findings, not exceptions to conceal.

There are distinct limits to the equilibrium claim and its portability. The threshold theorem uses primitive regularity, strict policy concavity, and polynomial sign restrictions on a specified interior duopoly branch. The corresponding \emph{global} policy equilibrium has been certified for one rational witness and a nonempty open primitive neighborhood, including deviations that lead to kink and monopoly continuations. We do not characterize global SPNE for arbitrary feasible primitives. The differentiated-Bertrand and partial-local-capture checks show that a local sign switch is not exclusive to the baseline formulation, but they are not global equilibrium theorems outside their interior and active-policy domains.

The welfare comparison has a different interpretation from a climate-policy recommendation. Subsidies can be transfers within the local objective, whereas territorial emissions enter as a reduced-form loss around a delegated target. The coordinated active-duopoly benchmark does not solve an unrestricted global climate-planner problem. In the canonical example, coordination increases modeled regional welfare \emph{and} territorial emissions as output expands. Neither the threshold theorem nor this benchmark establishes that policy coordination, subsidy expansion, or productive infrastructure necessarily reduces physical emissions. A broader carbon-price regime, cross-border damages, and national redistribution lie outside the model.

The stylized institutions matter as well. Plants do not relocate; firms are symmetric at the canonical configuration; infrastructure is a scalar productive input, not a congestible network; and there is neither a binding common fiscal budget nor explicit vertical allocation of policy authority. Japan's green-transformation initiatives motivate the coexistence of place-based infrastructure and firm-directed support, but they do not show that individual prefectures choose this exact pair of instruments independently. These limitations restrict institutional transfer, especially to location-bidding contests or programs in which central financing and local implementation are inseparable.

An empirical investigation would need to distinguish a \emph{threshold in policy responses} from ordinary spatial policy correlation. It would require measured product-market substitutability, rival policy shocks that can be separated from endogenous local matching, and a consistent infrastructure-response measure. The canonical curve even becomes more negative over part of the low-rivalry region before turning upward; imposing a single positive linear rivalry interaction would misstate the model. The paper supplies no causal estimate. Its contribution is to make the sign, relevant mechanisms, and observable restrictions explicit enough that future evidence could reject the prediction rather than merely illustrate it.
