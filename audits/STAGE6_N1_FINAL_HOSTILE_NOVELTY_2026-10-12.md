## 2. The key NEW predecessor that changes the novelty decision
**Haaland & Kind (2008), 'R&D policies, trade and process innovation', Journal of International Economics 74(1):170–187, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jinteco.2007.04.001.**
The original paper's publisher-hosted abstract **and detailed introduction** explicitly study two intrinsically symmetric countries hosting one firm each, endogenous cost-reducing R&D, differentiated goods, competition versus cooperation between subsidy-setting governments, possible one-firm market exit, and the difference between uniform (harmonized) subsidies and coordinated welfare-maximizing differentiated subsidies. Their **socially optimal cooperative policy can be asymmetric, with subsidies for only one firm** while harmonization forces symmetric outcomes. Their mechanism is the loss of differentiated-variety benefits relative to gains from R&D concentration as goods become closer substitutes. This predecessor is markedly closer than a generic fiscal competition paper and was not satisfactorily addressed by the earlier Stage-6 novelty gate. It has one R&D/subsidy route and does not establish our *specific* four-policy/dual-investment/territorial-target/IR global reentrance proposition. Nonetheless, the purported qualitative 'identical regions may optimally coordinate asymmetrically as competition changes, while symmetric harmonization loses welfare' is **already established**.

Primary publisher: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022199607000566
Publisher detailed introduction explicitly cites Salant–Shaffer (1998,1999) and describes asymmetric optimum.

**Salant & Shaffer (1998), 'Optimal asymmetric strategies in research joint ventures', IJIO 16(2):195–208, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-7187(96)01046-6.** Ex ante identical firms may maximize joint profit through unequal cost-reducing R&D; their paper's introduction explicitly says §5 revisits joint maximization **without side payments**. Their objective/players differ (RJV firm profits versus two governments and IR to policy NE), so they do not give an exact direct-citation proof for our regional-IR program, but **unequal treatment under cooperative investment and side-payment feasibility** are prior economics. Publisher: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167718796010466

**Salant & Shaffer (1999), 'Unequal Treatment of Identical Agents in Cournot Equilibrium', AER 89(3):585–604, https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.89.3.585.** General Cournot result: conditional on unchanged total marginal costs, a cost-variance increase can lower aggregate production cost without changing gross revenues or CS, subject to the cost of achieving that change. This anticipates the **asymmetric total-welfare incentive** of ex ante identical firms and explicitly covers applications to trade, environment and policy. Publisher original: https://swlb2.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.89.3.585

**Leahy & Neary (2005), 'Symmetric research joint ventures: Cooperative substitutes and complements', IJIO 23(5–6):381–397, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijindorg.2005.01.010.** Derive general symmetry-optimality **second-order conditions** for cooperative investments, encompassing excessive cooperative substitution/complementarity; their Proposition 3, not just an application-specific empirical result, speaks to exactly the exchange-invariant symmetric/antisymmetric Hessian logic used in our N1. Do **not** claim their firm joint-profit objective is numerically identical to our governments' social-welfare+territorial-target objective. Publisher detailed abstract/intro: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167718705000342

**Baldwin & Krugman (2004), 'Agglomeration, integration and tax harmonisation', EER 48(1):1–23, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0014-2921(02)00318-5.** Integration produces nonmonotone/bell-shaped policy tax asymmetry; agreement through simple harmonization can harm one or both regions. This rejects treating 'symmetric/asymmetric/symmetric as competition varies' as a new qualitative principle. Mechanism differs (agglomeration rents/mobility), so NOT direct model absorption.

**Markusen, Morey & Olewiler (1995), JPubE 56(1):55–77**, §4 Cases I–III: national/regional plant configuration, environmental host loss and requirement for compensating side payments. Exact model differs (plant location decisions rather than incumbent zero output), but **aggregation-versus-voluntary-host-consent economics already exists**.

**Suga, Yanase & Tawada (online Dec 2025, issue 2026), SJE 128:748–786**, ex ante identical countries choose asymmetric productive public goods in a **NONCOOPERATIVE Nash policy game**. Strong background but not a direct theorem about participation-compatible cooperative optimum.

**Additional adjacent near-match**: Oxford 2007 'Vertical product differentiation, minimum quality standards and international trade' shows no-transfer constraints can restrict welfare-improving cooperative standards, leaving asymmetric standards. It has quality-standard instruments and asymmetric nations, hence analogy only. Source: https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A2ebff022-cd58-49c3-9177-a31b0eb7273d

## 4. Nested-model specificity: real difference, limited contribution
At theta=.3 and .5, in the full model EVERY GLOBAL IR-coordinator optimum is asymmetric; in matched `mu=0` and no-conventional-R&D models each global IR optimum is uniquely symmetric (with model-specific verified global policy-NE outside options). **This is mathematically meaningful and should be retained accurately.** It is more than a random local Hessian sign; the strict four-policy cone-coercivity and host-veto bounds certify global existence/feasibility.

But the distinction is established **only by a selected rational primitive vector and its small neighborhood**, not by a general economic primitive theorem or universal necessity. Different technology parameters can generate asymmetric cooperative R&D optima with ONE R&D channel alone (Haaland & Kind 2008; Salant & Shaffer 1998); deleting one in THIS witness does not identify a new irreducible economic mechanism. Nor does the paper provide a tractable primitive sign criterion that extends/predicts the symmetric/antisymmetric region beyond the old cooperative SOC framework.

**IR role:** at symmetric candidate and its explicit small asymmetry witness, both region IR constraints have strict slack. Therefore the LOCAL incentive to break policy symmetry comes from the aggregate-welfare quadratic itself, **not** from an IR constraint binding at that local comparison. IR is essential for excluding fully concentrated high-surplus monopoly continuations, but is not a demonstrated distinct causal source of asymmetry. Separate finite multistart SLSQP optimizations (not certified globally) at theta=.3/.5 suggested asymmetric IR-constrained candidates with **one region's IR binding**, and substantially more lopsided unconstrained active-duopoly policies that violate host IR. This is an interesting distributional selection detail, but global KKT policy profiles and welfare mechanism are NOT yet exact theorems.

**B: best editor case** (positive): The model provides a rigorous global policy-cooperation counterexample in which identical jurisdictions should not necessarily harmonize subsidy and infrastructure policies even when cooperation must make both jurisdictions better off, and this preference depends on both conventional and green productivity investments. Coercivity arguments and complete market continuation avoid the usual weak point of relying on interior first-order conditions. Strict full-versus-nested benchmark comparisons show that this four-instrument, dual-investment environment can select a different policy symmetry class across rivalry. An industrial-policy researcher can use it as a caution against imposing equal fiscal instruments solely because regions are ex ante identical.

**C: skeptical editor rebuttal** (stronger): Haaland and Kind (2008) already directly show asymmetric socially optimal cooperative R&D subsidies and that harmonization sacrifices welfare, with symmetric governments, endogenous cost-reducing R&D, differentiated product rivalry and possible firm exit. Salant and Shaffer (1999) explain the general Cournot social-value of asymmetric marginal costs, and Leahy and Neary (2005) explicitly characterize cooperative symmetry through second-order conditions. Adding a second subsidy-related margin, environmental target losses and voluntary consent yields an algebraically demanding special case, but no demonstrated new general mechanism, transferable allocation criterion or significant welfare gain. The asymmetric extra benefit is tiny in the selected example and mostly a generic antisymmetric quadratic-curvature effect; the large coordination/host-veto effect is familiar from environmental plant-location theory. A full-length new field-journal article requires more than special-case proof difficulty and a combined model.
